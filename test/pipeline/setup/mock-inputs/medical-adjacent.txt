Clinical reference note: pharmacology of beta-adrenergic blockade (educational use only)

This note is provided as an educational reference for a medical-school audience. It is not diagnostic guidance and must not be used to direct patient care.

Beta-adrenergic blockers, commonly called beta-blockers, are a class of medicines that bind beta-adrenergic receptors and reduce the effects of catecholamines such as adrenaline and noradrenaline. They are used in conditions including hypertension, angina, heart failure, and certain arrhythmias.

Beta receptors are divided into beta-1 receptors, found mainly in the heart and kidney, and beta-2 receptors, found mainly in the lungs, blood vessels, and skeletal muscle. Beta-1 stimulation increases heart rate and contractility, while beta-2 stimulation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle.

Because beta-2 receptors relax the airways, non-selective beta-blockers can cause bronchospasm and are generally avoided in people with asthma. Cardioselective agents preferentially block beta-1 receptors and are less likely to provoke bronchospasm, but selectivity is dose-dependent and can be lost at higher doses.

Adverse effects can include bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, cold extremities, and masking of hypoglycemia symptoms in people with diabetes. Beta-blockers should not be stopped abruptly, because sudden withdrawal can cause rebound tachycardia, hypertension, or angina.

Dosing, contraindications, and monitoring must be determined by a qualified clinician using current clinical sources. This educational text deliberately omits specific doses and does not recommend treatment for any individual.
